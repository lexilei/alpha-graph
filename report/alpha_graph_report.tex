\title{Alpha-Graph: NLP-Driven Equity Signal Generation\\via SEC Filing Analysis --- A Negative Result}
\author{Zhihan Lei}
\date{April 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We present Alpha-Graph, a quantitative equity framework that extracts forward-looking information from SEC regulatory filings (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K) using TF-IDF text similarity, hand-coded 8-K event scoring, hidden Markov regime detection, a walk-forward LightGBM combiner, and an optional multi-agent LLM pipeline. The system was originally designed as a market-neutral long/short equity strategy. After running the full walk-forward backtest on 499 tickers across 168 months (2011--2026, $\sim$1.66 million ticker-months), the long/short construction does not work: the top10/bottom10 long/short portfolio achieves an annualized Sharpe of only $+0.32$ with a maximum drawdown of $-58.4\%$, statistically indistinguishable from a one-line 12-1 momentum factor (Sharpe $+0.31$). Eight alternative L/S variants --- rank-weighted, decile, vol-targeted, multi-factor, sector-neutral, regime-conditional, momentum-hedged, and a cross-factor hedge --- either match or worsen the baseline. The structural reason is a diagnostic finding that the Lazy Prices signal of Cohen, Malloy \& Nguyen (2020) is \emph{U-shaped, not monotonic}, in our 2011--2026 panel: the lowest-similarity decile (heavy filing rewriters) actually slightly outperforms the middle decile in subsequent months. The original short-side thesis of the Lazy Prices paper does not replicate. The only variant that survives is a long-only top-decile book (Method E), which delivers Sharpe $1.08$, annualized $+29.7\%$, and an annualized SPY-adjusted alpha of $+33.6\%$ (t-statistic $4.35$, p $= 2.3 \times 10^{-5}$, Mag~7 share $4.3\%$). We document the failure modes, the U-shape diagnostic, the cost-sensitivity analysis, the permutation test that retracts a previously claimed Anti-Momentum Sharpe of 1.91, and the conditions under which the long-only book remains a defensible weak alpha rather than disguised market exposure.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Public companies in the United States are required to file periodic disclosures with the SEC: annual reports (10-K), quarterly reports (10-Q), and material event disclosures (8-K). These filings contain information about business operations, risk factors, litigation, management changes, and financial performance that may be slow to incorporate into prices.

\citet{cohen2020lazy} documented the ``Lazy Prices'' anomaly: firms that substantially change the language of their annual filings subsequently underperform, suggesting that the market underreacts to textual information from regulatory disclosures. Their reported strategy --- long the most stable filers, short the heaviest rewriters --- earned approximately 188 basis points per month of risk-adjusted alpha in their sample.

This report documents an attempt to extend Cohen, Malloy \& Nguyen's framework to a 14-year panel of S\&P~500 constituents, with three additions:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{8-K event-driven signals}, scoring 25 different 8-K item types and exponentially weighting recent events. The signal updates approximately 40 times per year per ticker, versus once per year for 10-K filings.
    \item \textbf{Multi-agent LLM analysis} via a LangGraph fan-out/fan-in pipeline of specialized agents (Filing Analyst, News Synthesizer, Research Coordinator) backed by DeepSeek-V3.
    \item \textbf{Machine learning signal combination} via a walk-forward LightGBM model that combines filing text features, event scores, market regime, and price features to predict cross-sectional forward returns.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The result of this attempt is negative.} The long/short market-neutral strategy this work was originally built to deliver does not achieve a deployable Sharpe ratio in our 14-year out-of-sample window, and we show in Section~\ref{sec:ushape} that the failure is structural: the Lazy Prices signal is not monotonic in forward return in our panel, so any L/S construction that assumes monotonicity is fighting the data.

The report is organized to make the negative result transparent and reproducible. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data. Section~\ref{sec:signals} describes the signal generation pipeline (still in the codebase, unchanged). Section~\ref{sec:ml} describes the ML combiner. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the 14-year results across ten portfolio variants. Section~\ref{sec:ushape} presents the U-shape diagnostic that explains why the L/S variants fail. Section~\ref{sec:methode} presents the long-only Method~E result and its beta-attribution analysis. Section~\ref{sec:retraction} documents what previous versions of this report claimed and retracts the inflated numbers. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} lists the honest caveats. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{SEC Filings}

We download filings from the SEC EDGAR database for 499 S\&P~500 constituents over a 14-year window (2011--2026) using the \texttt{edgartools} library. For 10-K and 10-Q filings, we extract three sections: Item 1A (Risk Factors), Item 7 (MD\&A), and Item 1 (Business Description). For 8-K filings, we parse the item numbers from the filing metadata.

The full panel after filtering for tickers with usable text and price coverage contains approximately 1.66 million ticker-month observations across 168 monthly walk-forward folds.

\subsection{Market Data}

We obtain daily OHLCV price data via Yahoo Finance for 499 S\&P~500 constituents. Forward 21-trading-day returns serve as the prediction target. We also compute trailing 5-day and 21-day momentum, 21-day realized volatility, 60-day realized volatility, 12-1 momentum (12-month return excluding the most recent month), distance from 252-day high, and volume z-scores as auxiliary features.

A critical implementation detail: the \texttt{ret\_1d}, \texttt{ret\_5d}, and \texttt{ret\_21d} columns in our cached \texttt{market\_data.parquet} are \emph{forward}-looking (they are the prediction targets). When constructing backward-looking momentum and volatility features, we recompute them directly from \texttt{close} via \texttt{pct\_change}, never reusing the forward columns. This avoided a leakage bug that briefly inflated earlier results.

\subsection{Lazy Prices: TF-IDF Cosine Similarity}
\label{sec:lazy}

For each pair of consecutive 10-K filings $(d_{t-1}, d_t)$ from the same company, we compute TF-IDF vectors with bigrams, a 10{,}000-term vocabulary, and English stop-word removal, then take the cosine similarity:
\begin{equation}
    \text{sim}(d_{t-1}, d_t) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_{t-1} \cdot \mathbf{v}_t}{\|\mathbf{v}_{t-1}\| \, \|\mathbf{v}_t\|}.
\end{equation}
The mean similarity across 299 filing pairs in our sample is $0.896$ (std $0.204$), with extremes ranging from $0.10$ (Altria Group, near-complete rewrite) to $0.99$. Higher similarity means less change. Per the Cohen/Malloy/Nguyen thesis, higher similarity is the long signal. We assign cross-sectional quintile ranks: top quintile $\rightarrow +1$, bottom quintile $\rightarrow -1$.

The fundamental limitation of this signal --- which we knew from the start but underestimated --- is that it updates approximately once per year per ticker, so the cross-sectional rank is essentially stale for 11 of every 12 months.

\subsection{8-K Event-Driven Signal}
\label{sec:events}

To capture intra-year information, we score 8-K material event filings by item type. Each item has a hand-coded prior score reflecting expected market impact:

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

For each ticker we compute an exponentially weighted average with monthly decay $\lambda = 0.9$:
\begin{equation}
    s_i = \frac{\sum_{k} w_k \cdot \text{score}(e_k)}{\sum_k w_k}, \quad w_k = \lambda^{\Delta t_k / 30}
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t_k$ is the number of days since event $e_k$.

In the LightGBM combiner this is the highest-importance feature; the cosine similarity feature has zero importance. The 8-K event signal effectively subsumes the annual Lazy Prices signal once both are available.

\subsection{HMM Market Regime Detection}
\label{sec:regime}

We fit a Gaussian Hidden Markov Model on standardized market features (5-day return, 21-day vol, vol ratio, market breadth) to classify each trading day as Trending, Mean-Reverting, or Crisis. We use BIC-based model selection between 3- and 4-state variants and standardize features prior to fitting.

\subsection{Multi-Agent LLM Pipeline}

We deploy three LLM agents (DeepSeek-V3 via Together AI) in a fan-out/fan-in architecture using LangGraph:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Filing Analyst}: Compares the current and previous 10-K filing text, identifies material changes in risk factors and MD\&A, and produces a signal in $[-1, +1]$ with a confidence score. The prompt explicitly asks for both risk and opportunity to avoid bearish bias.
    \item \textbf{News Synthesizer}: Analyzes recent 8-K filings and categorizes events by materiality.
    \item \textbf{Research Coordinator}: Combines agent signals using confidence-weighted averaging with an agreement penalty.
\end{enumerate}

The Earnings Analyst is implemented but inactive (Finnhub free tier does not provide transcripts). The pipeline was used to produce a single one-period snapshot signal in \texttt{pipeline\_signals.parquet}; it is not part of the 14-year walk-forward backtest in \texttt{improvements.py}, which uses only the LightGBM combiner output. There is no longitudinal evaluation of the LLM pipeline because we never accumulated enough monthly snapshots.

\subsection{Walk-Forward Training}

To avoid lookahead bias, we train the model using a strict walk-forward protocol:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Training window}: 12 rolling months
    \item \textbf{Test window}: 1 calendar month
    \item \textbf{Purge gap}: 5 trading days between train end and test start
    \item \textbf{Target}: Forward 21-day return
    \item \textbf{Total folds}: 168 (2011--2026)
\end{itemize}

LightGBM hyperparameters are conservative: 15 leaves, learning rate 0.05, L1 = 0.1, L2 = 1.0, min child samples = 10. Critically, we set \texttt{deterministic=True} and \texttt{n\_jobs=1} so that walk-forward fits are bit-reproducible across runs and machines.

The headline is in row 1: the L/S baseline that this entire framework was designed to deliver achieves a 14-year out-of-sample Sharpe of $+0.32$ with a $-58\%$ maximum drawdown. That is worse than holding T-bills, with risk well above what any institutional allocator would tolerate.

The other things to notice in this table:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Pure 12-1 Momentum (Method G) gives Sharpe $+0.31$.} This is essentially the same number as our text-derived L/S signal. In other words, 14 years of work, $\sim$5,400 SEC filings parsed, and a multi-feature LightGBM model produce a long/short signal that is statistically indistinguishable from a one-line price-momentum factor any sophomore could implement in five minutes.
    \item \textbf{All multi-factor combinations are worse, not better.} Method D (Lazy + Mom + LowVol z-score average, rank-weighted) is Sharpe $-0.52$. Method H (Mom + LowVol) is $-0.43$. Method I (cross-factor hedge) is $-0.04$. The intuition that combining ``diversifying'' factors should improve Sharpe is wrong here, because the Lazy Prices signal carries no information beyond what momentum already provides, so averaging it in adds noise without alpha.
    \item \textbf{Method F (long top10 / short middle10) gets Sharpe $+0.61$,} almost double the standard top10/bot10. We discuss this in Section~\ref{sec:ushape}; it is a diagnostic confirmation of the U-shape problem rather than a deployable strategy.
    \item \textbf{Method E (long-only top10) gives Sharpe $+1.08$.} This is the only variant that survives, and the long-only construction is the punchline of this report (Section~\ref{sec:methode}).
\end{itemize}

\section{The U-Shape Diagnostic}
\label{sec:ushape}

When every reasonable variant of a strategy fails, the question is whether the failure is in the implementation or in the signal. We tested the latter directly. We binned all ticker-months in the 14-year panel into deciles by Lazy Prices signal strength and computed the average forward 21-day return per decile. The result:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Decile-by-decile forward return for the Lazy Prices signal in the full 14-year panel.}
\label{tab:ushape}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
Decile & Interpretation & Avg forward return / month \\
\midrule
9 (highest cosine sim, no filing changes) & strong long & $\mathbf{+2.10\%}$ \\
4--5 (middle, ambiguous) & neutral & $+1.19\%$ \\
0 (lowest cosine sim, filing rewritten) & ``strong short'' per Cohen/Malloy/Nguyen & $+1.37\%$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The original Lazy Prices paper assumes the bottom decile underperforms; that is the alpha that motivates shorting it. \textbf{In our 2011--2026 panel the bottom decile slightly outperforms the middle.} The signal is U-shaped, not monotonic.

The implications follow mechanically:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Rank-weighted L/S} (Method A) puts negative weight on the bottom decile, which has positive expected return. You are paying alpha to short a positive-EV bucket. Sharpe $-0.12$ --- exactly as predicted.
    \item \textbf{Top10/Bot10} has the same problem at smaller scale. The bot10 has slightly positive expected return; shorting it adds noise without alpha. The entire L/S Sharpe of $+0.32$ comes from the long top10, with the short side dragging it down.
    \item \textbf{Long top10 / Short MIDDLE10} (Method F) is Sharpe $+0.61$, almost double the top10/bot10. This confirms the U-shape diagnosis: shorting the actually-bad bucket (the middle) instead of the rewriter bucket helps. But ``long top10 / short middle10'' is not a defensible production strategy --- there is no economic reason to short the middle decile, and Sharpe $0.61$ is still well below any deployment threshold.
    \item \textbf{Long-only top10} (Method E) is Sharpe $+1.08$ because it drops the broken short side entirely. This is just acknowledging that the long signal works and the short signal doesn't.
\end{enumerate}

The short side of the original Cohen/Malloy/Nguyen finding does not replicate in our universe over 2011--2026. We do not have a definitive explanation for why; possibilities include (a) the post-2011 era differs structurally from their pre-GFC sample, (b) S\&P~500 large-caps behave differently from the broader universe their study used, (c) sample period overlap with quantitative crowding into NLP-driven equity strategies has eroded the original anomaly. Whatever the cause, the empirical fact in our panel is that the bottom decile slightly beats the middle, and once that is true no L/S construction that assumes monotonicity can work.

\section{The Method E Long-Only Result}
\label{sec:methode}

The one variant in Table~\ref{tab:improvements} with a Sharpe above 1 is Method E, the long-only top10 portfolio. This is just the long leg of the L/S baseline with the broken short side removed. We took it seriously enough to run a beta-attribution analysis (\texttt{backtest/attribution.py}) to check whether the result is real alpha or disguised market exposure.

\subsection{What Method E Is (and Isn't)}

Combining the beta attribution and concentration check, Method E is a defensible weak alpha as a long-side stock-picking signal. Specifically:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item Alpha t-stat $4.35$ exceeds the conventional $|t| > 2$ threshold by a wide margin.
    \item Beta to market is essentially zero, so the result is not just disguised SPY exposure.
    \item Holdings are not concentrated in Mag 7, so it's not disguised mega-cap quality.
    \item The signal works on the long side, where the underlying Lazy Prices intuition (companies that don't change their filings are stable) is the original Cohen/Malloy/Nguyen finding that does seem to replicate.
\end{itemize}

But with several caveats that should be kept in mind:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{It is not the long/short market-neutral strategy this project was built to deliver.} A long-only book is a different product with different risk and capital implications.
    \item \textbf{Survivorship bias is unmeasured.} The universe is current S\&P~500 constituents; companies removed from the index over 2011--2026 (typically after underperformance) are excluded. This mechanically inflates long-side returns by an unknown amount, possibly substantial.
    \item \textbf{The 14-year annualized $29.7\%$ has to be compared against SPY's $13.7\%$ over the same window.} The spread is $16$ percentage points and the SPY-adjusted alpha t-stat is $4.35$, so the spread is unlikely to be pure noise --- but a single 14-year long book of US equities outperforming SPY is not by itself proof of alpha, and it is consistent with a quality/momentum tilt that we have only partially stripped out.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Retraction of Previous Numbers}
\label{sec:retraction}

Earlier versions of this report claimed substantially better results:
\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{``ML Combiner Sharpe 1.77, +80.5\% over 24 months.''} This was generated on a cherry-picked Mar~2024--Feb~2026 window. On the actual cached \texttt{walk\_forward\_results.parquet} for that period, the L/S strategy is $-44.9\%$ cumulative with Sharpe $-2.64$. The headline number was inconsistent with the cached data and is not reproducible. It is retracted.
    \item \textbf{``Anti-Momentum Features Sharpe 1.91 over 18 months.''} This was a structural extension that added 52-week-high distance and 5-day reversal as additional ML features and was claimed as the production strategy in the previous version of this document. We ran a permutation test (\texttt{backtest/permutation\_test.py}, results in \texttt{permutation\_test\_null.parquet}) that jointly shuffles the two features within each month and re-runs the entire walk-forward training pipeline. The null distribution has mean $+0.016$, std $0.22$, range $[-0.37, +0.47]$. The 18-month subset on which the $1.91$ headline was generated happens to be the period (post-Jan-2025) when momentum reversal worked. On the full 14-year panel the same approach does not stand out from the null. The original headline relied on the lucky window. It is retracted.
    \item \textbf{The post-hoc risk-management overlays} (Momentum Protection, Regime Sizing, Combined) and \textbf{the structural extensions} (Sector-Neutral, Regime-Conditional, Momentum Hedge) were all evaluated only on the same 24-month window and inherit the cherry-picking problem. Their headline Sharpes (1.94, 1.60, 1.91, 1.67, 1.47, 1.77) are not robust when re-evaluated on the 14-year panel and should be treated as post-hoc tuning artifacts rather than genuine improvements.
\end{itemize}

These claims are no longer made by this report. The code that generated them is still in the repository (\texttt{backtest/extensions.py}, \texttt{backtest/permutation\_test.py}, \texttt{trading/signal\_generator.py}) so that the retraction is reproducible. The \texttt{trading/} layer still runs an ``Anti-Momentum'' production wiring; readers should treat that wiring as a research artifact, not a deployable strategy.

\section{Honest Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{The L/S strategy doesn't work.} Sharpe $0.32$, max drawdown $-58\%$, breakeven cost $\sim$75 bps/month. This is the central finding of this report.
    \item \textbf{The long-only Method E result has alpha $\mathbf{t = 4.35}$ but lives with survivorship bias.} The universe is current S\&P~500 constituents; companies removed during the panel are excluded. Backfilling historical S\&P~500 membership would be the right next step, but we did not do it. Any reader should mentally discount the $+29.7\%$ annualized accordingly until that experiment is run.
    \item \textbf{The earnings transcript signal was never tested.} Finnhub free tier doesn't include transcripts, so the Earnings Analyst agent is built but inactive. Academic literature suggests transcripts are the most likely source of additional alpha (\citealt{kim2025earnings} report 247 bps from transcript tone analysis), and we never got to evaluate it.
    \item \textbf{The multi-agent LLM pipeline was never longitudinally evaluated.} It produces sensible per-ticker scores in a one-period snapshot, but we never accumulated enough monthly snapshots to compute its IC or run a walk-forward backtest of its output. Whatever the LLM agents are doing is currently undemonstrated as alpha.
    \item \textbf{The knowledge graph spillover signal hurt OOS returns and is disabled.} It is implemented in \texttt{signals/graph\_signal.py} and \texttt{data/relationships.py} (LLM extracts inter-company relationships from 10-K Business Description sections, then propagates 8-K event scores along the graph), but every variant we tried that included it as a feature in the LightGBM combiner reduced Sharpe.
    \item \textbf{No transaction-cost realism for the bottom decile.} 20 bps/month is plausible for top-of-book S\&P~500 names but not for the bottom decile, which often includes illiquid and hard-to-borrow stocks. The cost sensitivity in Table~\ref{tab:costs} shows that realistic costs (50--75 bps/month) take the L/S Sharpe to zero or negative.
    \item \textbf{Determinism only inside LightGBM, not across the full pipeline.} The LightGBM model is bit-reproducible (\texttt{deterministic=True}, \texttt{n\_jobs=1}), but yfinance API responses, SEC EDGAR responses, and the LLM agent calls are not deterministic, so reproducing the exact numbers in this report requires re-using the cached parquet files.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to build a long/short market-neutral equity strategy from SEC filings. After running the walk-forward backtest on 14 years of data and 499 tickers, the L/S strategy does not work. The Sharpe is $0.32$, the max drawdown is $-58\%$, and a one-line 12-1 momentum factor produces an essentially identical $+0.31$ Sharpe with no SEC filing data at all. Adding ``diversifying'' factors makes things worse, not better. The structural reason is that the Lazy Prices signal is U-shaped, not monotonic, in our 2011--2026 panel: the lowest-similarity decile slightly outperforms the middle decile in subsequent months, so the original Cohen/Malloy/Nguyen short-side thesis does not replicate, and any L/S construction that assumes monotonicity fights the data.

The one variant that survives is the long-only top decile. It delivers an annualized $+29.7\%$ over 14 years with a SPY-adjusted alpha of $+33.6\%$ (t-stat $4.35$, p $= 2.3 \times 10^{-5}$), beta $-0.19$, residual Sharpe $1.21$, and a Mag~7 share of only $4.3\%$. This is a defensible weak alpha as a long-side stock-picking signal, but it is not the market-neutral product the project was built to deliver, it carries unmeasured survivorship bias, and a single long book of US equities outperforming SPY over a 14-year window is not by itself definitive.

Two earlier headline numbers --- ``ML Combiner Sharpe 1.77 over 24 months'' and ``Anti-Momentum Sharpe 1.91 over 18 months'' --- are formally retracted in Section~\ref{sec:retraction}. They were generated on cherry-picked windows, did not survive when re-evaluated on the full panel or against a permutation null, and were inconsistent with the cached results files for the same periods. The code that produced them is still in the repository so the retraction is reproducible.

The rest of the codebase (data ingestion, signal generation, multi-agent LLM pipeline, regime detection, ML combiner, paper-trading wiring) is unchanged. This document is the honest accounting of what those components actually deliver on the data we have.
